## I. What Happened

### A weak US jobs report flipped the Fed toward a September rate cut — and sent gold to a record. This, not the war, is the market's driver now.
The single most important development for the reader's portfolio is monetary, not military. **A cooling US labour market has inverted the Fed narrative toward rate cuts.** June payrolls came in weak at +57,000 (unemployment 4.2%), the July report was due Friday 7 August with soft expectations (~83,000), and Citi now calls for three cuts through January 2027 — a sharp turn from the FOMC of only two weeks ago, which held with three members dissenting *for a hike*. Markets have moved to price a **September rate cut**, the two-year yield falling and the curve steepening (BLS, CNBC, market data). **Gold responded with a record, jumping 2.9% to ~$4,366, silver +5%, the whole precious complex ripping** — and crucially it did so on a *de-escalation* day, confirming gold trades on rates and the Fed, not the war. *(The specific August-payroll figure circulating in some commentary could not be independently verified against the BLS July release and is not asserted here; the confirmed driver is the cooling-labour trend and the market's rate-cut repricing.)* The forward significance is the tailwind: a US rate cut is a classic positive for emerging markets and India — softer dollar, resumed capital inflows — landing precisely as India's own picture turns uniformly constructive (below). The only thing that reverses it is hot inflation data — PPI Wednesday, CPI Thursday.

### Iran and Oman reached a Strait of Hormuz route agreement — the war's most concrete diplomatic result, but on Iran's terms, not Trump's.
The third pause has now held roughly nine days — Trump declined on 1 August to follow through on the threatened bombing campaign, and there have been no US–Iran strikes since 29 July — and it produced something real. **Iran announced on 5 August that it had reached a bilateral agreement with Oman on a Hormuz shipping route: vessels enter the Persian Gulf through an Iranian-controlled route and exit through an Oman-controlled route** (Bloomberg). It is the most concrete diplomatic step of the entire war. But read the fine print, because it is the whole story: the agreement **institutionalises the Iranian control Tehran has demanded all along, and does not by itself reopen the strait** — Iran insists the United States must first meet additional conditions, and CNN's framing is blunt: "an agreement is taking shape, but *not one Trump wants*." NBC: "one or both would have to back down." So the sovereignty gap that collapsed every prior cycle is narrowed to a US–Iran conditions dispute, not closed — and whether Trump accepts an arrangement leaving Iran controlling inbound traffic is the binary the whole de-escalation now turns on.

### India is now the strongest setup of the cycle — the RBI held and cut its inflation forecast, oil is below $85, the rupee is firm, and the Fed is turning dovish.
Every vector that mattered for India has broken favourably at once. At its **5 August meeting the RBI held the repo rate at 5.25% unanimously and *lowered* its inflation forecast to 5.0%** (from 5.1%), keeping growth at 6.7% (Forbes India) — a stable, constructive outcome markets took as "RBI-led stability." Layer that onto the favourable 10% tariff outcome, **Brent sustained below $85** (import-bill and current-account relief), a **rupee firm at ₹95.27**, and the new **Fed tailwind** toward cuts, and India has an unusually clean hand: the Nifty is consolidating its rally around 24,545 (+2.4% on the month) with the broader market outperforming. The domestic macro risk set — tariff, oil, rupee, RBI — has cleared entirely; what remains is only the war's low-probability tail (a pause collapse). For an investor whose primary target is Indian equities, this is the brightest the case has looked all cycle.

### The nuclear firebreak holds — now a bargaining chip — while Lebanon and Israel talk on, still split.
Two quieter threads. No August strike has touched any Iranian nuclear site; the enrichment complex remains untouched through the pause, and the file is now embedded in the diplomacy — the Hormuz deal is explicitly "aimed at restarting nuclear negotiations," so denuclearization is a negotiating goal, not a strike target (NBC). Separately, **Lebanon and Israel concluded a seventh round of direct, US-mediated talks in Rome (4–6 August), still split on the core issue of disarming Hezbollah** — which says it will pull back south of the Litani but refuses to disarm north of it, its leader calling the talks "nothing but shame" (Middle East Monitor, Al Jazeera). Seven rounds of *direct* Lebanon–Israel negotiation is itself a real de-escalation on the northern front, but it remains process, not settlement. And the Saudi–Houthi front, kinetic in late July, has been in a lull with no confirmed fresh strikes.

### Oil's war premium is gone — but the shipping-insurance market still hasn't signed off on the peace.
Brent sits at **$83.24, down 17% over three months**, the war premium fully unwound on the pause, the Oman route agreement, and OPEC+ September barrels; oil is no longer a day-to-day crisis instrument. Yet the honest dissent comes from the slowest, most rigorous market: **war-risk insurance for Gulf transits is still 3–10% of hull value ($3–10M per voyage, up to ~$21M for a high-tension Hormuz crossing), versus ~0.25% pre-war — it has not normalized** even as the route agreement forms (Lloyd's List, Strauss Center). "Market pricing is not adjusting as conditions improve." Insurers re-rate down only on operationally-confirmed sustained reopening, which lags any deal by weeks — so the insurance premium remains the cleanest tell that this is not yet a settlement. Tanker and LNG equities (Frontline +4.2%, Cheniere +4.3%) rose on the reopening-odds trade.
